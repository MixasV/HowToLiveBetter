# Addendum: Occupational Disease Protection, Chemical Burns, and Blue-Light Protection · Verification Record (2026-09-08)

Task source: the reader raised three things — "Is there anything on treating chemical burns, like contact with acid water"; "Some factories in China have no safety measures at all, many accidents, and also dust, causing aftereffects that last for decades"; "And is there any harm in looking at screens? They are all strong light, and after three months of work my eyes basically cannot see clearly."

Existing coverage: chemical burns had only half an item — Section 13, item 12 (added in the previous round) writes about rinsing skin and eyes for 15 minutes, but the whole item is written as "household cleaning agents and accidental ingestion", without the workplace, removal of contaminated clothing, safety data sheets, and employer responsibility; the scald item in Section 13, item 6 only covers thermal burns. Occupational disease was completely blank: searching the whole book for "occupational disease" appears only in the citation of the work-related injury regulations in Section 19 and one TODO in Section 23, and "pneumoconiosis", "dust", "noise", and "eye protection" return zero hits. Screens and eyes were also completely blank: "visual fatigue", "dry eye", "blue light", and "myopia" return zero hits.

**The reader's third statement must be corrected in the main text**: ordinary screens will not make a person "basically unable to see clearly after three months of work". What screens cause is digital eye strain and dry eye, which are reversible; the strong light that causes irreversible eye damage in the short term is welding arc light, ultraviolet, and lasers, and that is an occupational hazard, corresponding to protective goggles rather than blue-light-blocking lenses. So this item is written as "which kind of light really harms the eyes and which does not", placed in the negative list.

| <https://niohp.chinacdc.cn/kpdw/202507/t20250722_308659.htm> | Yes | Skin contact "immediately rinse the contact area with plenty of clean water for at least 15 minutes"; eye contact "immediately rinse the eyes with plenty of clean water or normal saline for at least 15 minutes … open the eyelids to ensure the water flow fully contacts the eyeball and the inner side of the eyelids, do not rub the eyes. Go to ophthalmology immediately after rinsing" |
| <http://yjt.hunan.gov.cn/yjt/tszt/aqzs/201509/t20150919_3113778.htm> | Yes | "For poisons absorbed through the skin, or corrosive poisons that cause skin burns, the contaminated clothing should be removed immediately, and plenty of clean water should be used for rinsing, lukewarm water may also be used, hot water is prohibited"; another passage "rinse the wound with plenty of flowing clean water for 20 to 30 minutes (longer for strong chemicals)" and "do not arbitrarily apply ointment or red mercurochrome to the fresh wound, and do not wrap it with dirty cloth" |

Set at B: the rinsing durations given by the two official materials are 15 minutes and 20 to 30 minutes, two different approaches, and there is no unified national standard figure, so the main text states that the longer one is taken and the remark explains the discrepancy.

Three deliberate wording choices: (1) "do not casually grab another acid or alkali to neutralize" — the Hunan material writes "plenty of clean water or other prepared detoxifying or neutralizing liquid", referring to the special rinsing liquid prepared in advance by the unit, not on-site counter-neutralization, and the remark points out this distinction; (2) for dry lime powder and cement powder, brush it off first and then rinse — some local national defense mobilization office pages further claim on this basis that "concentrated sulfuric acid also cannot be rinsed with water immediately; first wipe it dry with a clean cloth strip", but the approaches differ across places and there is a lack of authoritative basis, so the main text does not write this, and the remark notes it; (3) the legal side connects to "chemical skin burns" and "chemical eye burns" in the occupational disease catalogue and to Article 37 of the Occupational Disease Prevention and Control Law, under which the employer bears the treatment costs.
