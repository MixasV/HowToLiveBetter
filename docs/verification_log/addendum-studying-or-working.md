# Addendum: Studying or Going to Work After Middle School · Verification Record (2026-09-10)

Task source: the reader asked "Is it better to keep studying, or to go to work right after middle school", and cited two common sayings — "some people with no education become bosses" and "some 985 graduates deliver food".

Existing coverage: the whole book had only the original item 1 of Section 23 (the global return to education of 9%) touching on this, and it explicitly says "this is not a specific figure for China, and can only serve as an order-of-magnitude reference". Searching for "child labor", "compulsory education", "grant", "student loan", "secondary vocational", and "Vocational Education Law" under book/ all returned zero hits; "survivorship bias" appears only once in the remark of the original item 6 of Section 23, discussing recruitment platform wage figures, and does not cover educational qualifications. In other words, the layer of "should I keep studying" is completely blank, and the original Section 23 assumes the reader has already decided to "learn something" and only answers "what to learn".

Placement: 6 items are added in front of Section 23, and the original items 1 to 7 shift to items 7 to 13, with the section introduction rewritten as "the first six items answer whether to keep studying, and the last seven answer how to choose". It is placed in Section 23 rather than a new section because these six items and the last seven share the same ruler (how much money and time are spent, how much income is gained back), and splitting them would require repeating the explanation of the return to education on both sides. The whole book goes from 473 to 479 items, level A from 301 to 306, level B from 123 to 124, level C unchanged at 49, literature links from 918 to 927, dispute notes from 43 to 44, and TODO unchanged at 36.

## Item 2 (years of education and adult mortality)

| URL | Rechecked | Original quoted passage |
|---|---|---|
| <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(23)00306-7> (Europe PMC REST retrieved the bibliographic record and full abstract, PMID 38278172, The Lancet Public Health 2024;9(3):e155-e165) | Yes | "an average reduction in mortality risk of 1·9% (95% uncertainty interval 1·8-2·0) per additional year of education"; "2·9% (2·8-3·0) … for adults aged 18-49 years, compared with a 0·8% (0·6-1·0) reduction for adults older than 70 years"; "603 of which were eligible for analysis and included data from 70 locations in 59 countries, producing a final dataset of 10 355 observations"; "We found no differential effect of education on all-cause mortality by sex or Socio-demographic Index level"; "We identified publication bias (p<0·0001)" |

The author is recorded as "IHME-CHAIN Collaborators" according to Europe PMC's authorString, without writing an individual first author. Set at level A (disputed): the effect size, interval, and sample size are complete, but the authors themselves report publication bias and high heterogeneity, the included studies are all observational, and education cannot be separated from family conditions as a confounder, as stated in the remark. Benefit magnitude "large" — the mortality approach's threshold by relative reduction does not apply (1.9% per year is a single-year increment, and studying to high school is three more years than to middle school, accumulating to about 5%–6%), so here it is judged as large on the basis of "the additional years of study are usually counted as three years, and 2.9% per year for the 18-49 age group", and the reason is recorded here. Cost money=0 (tuition is counted separately in item 4) time=a lot willpower=yes, composite "average" — this is honest: studying several more years is inherently one of the highest-cost actions in this book.
