# 2. Don't Die Slowly

This section only includes chronic risk factors with large effects on overall mortality and strong evidence, ranked by cost-effectiveness from highest to lowest. Unless specifically noted as randomized trials, all numbers come from observational studies. HR/RR contain confounding and reverse causation; read them as "direction and magnitude" rather than "discount you can get by following instructions."

### 1. Quit smoking, the sooner the better
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Willpower during cessation period (weeks to months); money is actually saved — one pack a day costs about $3–$4.5, quitting saves this.
- Say it in plain language: Smokers live on average more than ten years less than non-smokers; quitting before age 40 can eliminate about 90% of the death risk from continuing to smoke; the earlier you quit, the more years you recover.
- Benefit: US cohort (observational): current smokers have life expectancy more than 10 years shorter than never-smokers; quitting before 40 can eliminate about 90% of the death risk from continuing to smoke; quitting at ages 25–34, 35–44, and 45–54 gains about 10, 9, and 6 years respectively (reported as life-years in original). Chinese cohort (observational): 2010s urban male smokers overall mortality RR 1.65, rural males RR 1.22; after 10 years of active quitting, attributable risk approaches zero.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128>; Chen Z et al. (2015). Contrasting male and female trends in tobacco-attributed mortality in China: evidence from successive nationwide prospective cohort studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)00340-2>
- Note: Secondhand smoke is also lethal: in 2004, approximately 603,000 people worldwide died from secondhand smoke exposure, accounting for about 1% of total deaths that year (Oberg M et al. (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>). Even if you don't smoke, avoid others' smoke, especially don't let children breathe it.

### 2. Don't smoke at home or in the car, and don't let guests smoke indoors
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0; the hard part is talking to family and guests about it
- Say it in plain language: Globally about 600,000 people die each year from breathing other people's smoke, nearly 30% of them children; long-term secondhand smoke exposure raises risks of high blood pressure, heart disease, and stroke by about 30%, and smoke inhaled at home is more harmful than outside.
- Benefit: In 2004, 603,000 people worldwide died from secondhand smoke, about 1.0% of global deaths; 28% were children; in a meta-analysis of 57 studies, secondhand smoke exposure was associated with hypertension OR 1.28, heart disease 1.39, myocardial infarction 1.50, stroke 1.36, and home exposure carried higher risk than non-home exposure.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Öberg M, Jaakkola MS, Woodward A, Peruga A, Prüss-Ustün A (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet: "603,000 deaths were attributable to second-hand smoke in 2004, which was about 1·0% of worldwide mortality. 47% of deaths from second-hand smoke occurred in women, 28% in children, and 26% in men", "61% of DALYs were in children". <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>; (2026). The Associations Between Secondhand Smoke Exposure and Various Cardiovascular Diseases: A Meta-Analysis. Nicotine & Tobacco Research: 57 studies, "hypertension (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.15 to 1.40), heart disease (OR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.28 to 1.50), myocardial infarction (OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.17 to 1.84), stroke (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.18 to 1.54)" "Home exposure has a higher risk of CVD than non-home exposure". <https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf111>
- Note: Home is the place most worth controlling, not only because you spend the most time there, but also because the meta-analysis shows home exposure effects are larger than non-home exposure. Children bear 61% of secondhand smoke DALY losses. See item 1 in this section for quitting smoking yourself.

### 3. Don't drink sugary beverages
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Zero; switching to plain water or unsweetened tea actually saves money.
- Say it in plain language: People who drink more than two bottles of sweetened drinks per day have about 20% higher death probability over the same period compared to those who rarely drink them; one to two bottles per day raises it by over 10%.
- Benefit: Two large US cohorts (observational, about 118,000 people, 36,000 deaths): ≥2 servings/day vs <1 serving/month, overall mortality HR 1.21; 1–2 servings/day HR 1.14. European EPIC cohort (observational, 450,000 people): ≥2 cups/day vs <1 cup/month, sugary soft drinks HR 1.08, all soft drinks combined HR 1.17.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Malik VS et al. (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401>; Mullee A et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Note: Observational; heavy drinkers generally have worse overall lifestyles, so the effect may be overestimated. In EPIC, artificially sweetened drinks ≥2 cups/day had HR 1.26, actually higher than sugary drinks, likely reverse causation (people with existing metabolic disease switching to diet drinks). Don't treat diet drinks as a safe replacement to drink in large quantities — water should be the first choice.

### 4. Don't chew betel nut
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0, and you save money not buying betel nut
- Say it in plain language: People who chew betel nut have about 20% higher death probability over the same period compared to those who don't; diabetes and metabolic syndrome risks are nearly 50% higher; oral and esophageal cancers are counted separately.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of 17 Asian studies, 388,000 people: betel nut chewers relative to non-chewers had overall mortality relative risk 1.21 (P=0.02, 179,600 people), diabetes 1.47, metabolic syndrome 1.51; betel nut itself is a known risk factor for oral and esophageal cancer.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS One, 8(8), e70679. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>
- Note: Oral cancer risk is proportional to years chewed and daily amount; risk decreases over time after quitting. Betel nut containing tobacco is even more dangerous.

### 5. Switch your household salt to low-sodium salt (potassium salt)
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: under a dollar more per bag than regular salt; taste is almost identical, no willpower needed.
- Say it in plain language: In a randomized trial of 20,000 people, those who switched to low-sodium salt had about 12% lower death probability over five years and about 14% lower stroke risk. This result from randomized grouping is more trustworthy than typical observational data.
- Benefit: Randomized trial (rural China, 20,995 people with stroke history or hypertension ≥60 years old, 4.74-year follow-up): low-sodium salt group vs regular salt group, overall mortality RR 0.88, stroke RR 0.86, major cardiovascular events RR 0.87; hyperkalemia events showed no significant difference between groups.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675>
- Note: Controversy. PURE cohort (observational, O'Donnell M et al. (2014). Urinary sodium and potassium excretion, mortality, and cardiovascular events. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889>) estimated sodium excretion <3 g/day had mortality+cardiovascular event composite OR 1.27, ≥7 g/day OR 1.15, suggesting a J-shaped curve for sodium intake. But SSaSS is a randomized trial, and low-sodium salt is only a partial replacement that won't push sodium that low. The trial population was high-risk elderly; healthy young people get smaller absolute benefits; people with impaired kidney function or taking potassium-sparing medications should consult a doctor before switching.

### 6. Brush teeth carefully, clean between teeth once a day, replace missing teeth promptly
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Floss or interdental brushes cost $5–15 per year, adding two to three minutes per day; professional cleaning costs $15–30 each time.
- Say it in plain language: People who use dental floss or interdental brushes daily have about 10% lower death probability over the same period; elderly people who have lost all their teeth have about 90% higher death probability; those with fewer than 20 teeth remaining have about double.
- Benefit: Japanese 9,676-person 6-year cohort: users of interdental cleaning tools had overall mortality hazard ratio 0.89, tongue cleaner users 0.77; meta-analysis of community elderly: fully edentulous mortality OR 1.87 (95% CI 1.35–2.59), fewer than 20 teeth 2.04 (1.67–2.49).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Wang K, Matsuyama Y, Kiuchi S, et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789>; Ko MJ, Seo S, So JS, et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>
- Note: Controversy. Observational data; people with poor teeth often have worse overall health and economic status, so the causal direction is uncertain. But the cost is extremely low, and periodontal disease and tooth loss directly affect eating.

### 7. Walk 7,000–8,000 steps daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: About 60–90 minutes of walking, can be built into commuting and errands; no money needed.
- Say it in plain language: People who walk 5,800 steps daily have about 40% lower death probability over the same period compared to those walking only 3,500 steps; 7,800 steps brings it down about 45%, after which gains level off. For elderly people, 6,000–8,000 steps is enough.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of 15 cohorts (observational, 47,471 people, 3,013 deaths): by step quartiles, median 5,801, 7,842, 10,901 steps/day vs 3,553 steps/day, overall mortality HR 0.60, 0.55, 0.47 respectively; for ≥60 years, benefits level off at 6,000–8,000 steps, for <60 years at 8,000–10,000 steps. Another meta-analysis: effectiveness starts from about 3,867 steps/day, each additional 1,000 steps/day reduces overall mortality by about 15%.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Paluch AE et al. (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9>; Banach M et al. (2023). The association between daily step count and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: a meta-analysis. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwad229>
- Note: Observational; the lowest step counts include already-sick and frail people (reverse causation), so HR tends to be inflated; but the dose-response is clear, with the greatest marginal gain from 4,000 to 7,000 steps — no need to obsess over 10,000 steps. This and item 7 are two ways of measuring the same thing; meeting either target is sufficient.

### 8. If you have high blood pressure or high cholesterol, take medication as prescribed, don't stop on your own
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Generic blood pressure and cholesterol drugs cost $1–15 per month; one daily habit.
- Say it in plain language: Each 10 mmHg reduction in blood pressure lowers death probability by about 13% and stroke by about 27%; statins lowering LDL cholesterol by one unit reduce death probability by about 10%. People who take medication on time have 30%–45% lower death probability than those who frequently miss doses.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of randomized trials: each 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure, overall mortality RR 0.87, major cardiovascular events RR 0.80, stroke RR 0.73, heart failure RR 0.72; statins lowering LDL by 1.0 mmol/L, overall mortality RR 0.90, major vascular events RR 0.78. Adherence meta-analysis (observational): statin and antihypertensive adherence ≥80% vs poor adherence, overall mortality RR 0.55 and 0.71 respectively.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8>; Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) Collaboration (2010). Efficacy and safety of more intensive lowering of LDL cholesterol: a meta-analysis of data from 170 000 participants in 26 randomised trials. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61350-5>; Chowdhury R et al. (2013). Adherence to cardiovascular therapy: a meta-analysis of prevalence and clinical consequences. European Heart Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht295>
- Note: Only applies to people with medical indications for these medications; healthy people don't need them. The adherence numbers are observational — "people who stick to medication are more disciplined to begin with" inflates the effect; the first two sets of randomized trial numbers are more reliable. This section does not single out blood sugar control: evidence for intensive glucose lowering on overall mortality is less consistent than for blood pressure and cholesterol.

### 9. Sleep about 7 hours per night, keep a regular schedule
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Time (for most people, replacing phone scrolling with sleep); keeping a regular schedule requires a little self-discipline.
- Say it in plain language: People who consistently sleep less than 7 hours have about 10% higher death probability over the same period; the more irregular the schedule, the higher the risk — the most regular sleepers have 20%–nearly 50% lower risk than the most irregular. Regularity matters more than duration.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis (observational, 16 studies, 1.38 million people, 113,000 deaths): short sleepers overall mortality RR 1.12, long sleepers RR 1.30. Dose-response meta-analysis: using 7 hours as the lowest point, less than 7 hours each hour less RR 1.06, more than 7 hours each hour more RR 1.13. UK Biobank about 61,000 people with wrist-worn device data (observational): most regular sleep quartile vs least regular, overall mortality 20%–48% lower; regularity is a better predictor of death than duration.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep duration and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585>; Yin J et al. (2017). Relationship of Sleep Duration With All-Cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Events: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies. JAHA. <https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.117.005947>; Windred DP et al. (2024). Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration: A prospective cohort study. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad253>
- Note: The high risk of long sleep is mostly reverse causation (depression, chronic disease, sleep apnea make people sleep more); no need to deliberately compress sleep. Short sleep and irregularity are more worth changing. Regularity evidence currently comes from a single cohort, rated B on its own.

### 10. Accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week; brisk walking counts
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: 20–45 minutes daily; no money needed.
- Say it in plain language: People who brisk-walk 150–300 minutes per week have about 30% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-exercisers; even if you don't hit the full target, you still get 20% lower risk; reaching 3–5 times the target floors the benefit at about 40% reduction, beyond which there's no further decline but also no harm.
- Benefit: Multi-cohort pooled analysis (observational): achieving guideline minimum 1–2× (7.5–15 MET·h/week, roughly 150–300 minutes brisk walking/week) vs sedentary, overall mortality HR 0.69; below the minimum still HR 0.80; at 3–5× HR 0.61 plateaus, more doesn't decrease further but is harmless (≥10× HR 0.69). Accelerometer-measured meta-analysis: highest quartile of moderate-to-vigorous activity vs lowest HR 0.52.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality: a detailed pooled analysis of the dose-response relationship. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533>; Ekelund U et al. (2019). Dose-response associations between accelerometry measured physical activity and sedentary time and all cause mortality: systematic review and harmonised meta-analysis. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>
- Note: Observational; accelerometer studies have shorter follow-up and older samples, HR contains reverse causation components, real effects likely smaller than 0.52 magnitude. Either this or item 4 is sufficient.

### 11. Play racket sports three times a week, 45 minutes each time
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=medium willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Court fees of $5–15 per session; about 2 hours per week.
- Say it in plain language: People who regularly play tennis, badminton, or table tennis have about 50% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-players, and about 56% lower cardiovascular death; swimming is about 28% lower; running and soccer showed no difference in this data.
- Benefit: UK 80,306-person cohort: compared to non-participants, racket sports (tennis, badminton, table tennis) overall mortality hazard ratio 0.53 (95% CI 0.40–0.69), cardiovascular death 0.44 (0.24–0.83); swimming 0.72 and 0.59; aerobic dance 0.73 and 0.64; cycling 0.85; running and soccer showed no significant association.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Oja P, Kelly P, Pedisic Z, et al. (2017). Associations of specific types of sports and exercise with all-cause and cardiovascular-disease mortality: a cohort study of 80 306 British adults. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 51(10), 812-817. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096822>
- Note: Controversy. Observational study; people who play racket sports are generally healthier and more socially active; the non-significant running association also suggests selection effects. Don't conclude that running is useless — this section has separate entries on total exercise volume.

### 12. Accumulate 4–5 minutes daily of vigorous intermittent activities like climbing stairs or walking fast
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0; no extra workout time needed.
- Say it in plain language: People who normally never exercise can reduce their death probability by about 40% and cardiovascular death by nearly half just by having three one-to-two-minute bursts of vigorous activity (climbing stairs, walking fast) each day, compared to those with none at all.
- Benefit: UK Biobank 25,200 non-exercisers, average 6.9-year follow-up, 852 deaths: three daily episodes of 1–2 minutes of vigorous intermittent activity reduced overall mortality and cancer mortality by 38%–40% and cardiovascular mortality by 48%–49% compared to none; accumulating 4.4 minutes daily reduced overall and cancer mortality by 26%–30%, cardiovascular mortality by 32%–34%.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Stamatakis E, Ahmadi MN, Gill JMR, et al. (2022). Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine, 28, 2521-2529. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x>
- Note: Controversy. Measured with wearable devices, more reliable than questionnaires, but still observational with only 6.9-year follow-up. Study subjects are people who never exercise; doesn't apply to regular exercisers.

### 13. Do 30–60 minutes of strength training per week
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=medium willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: 1–2 times per week, 20–30 minutes each; bodyweight squats and push-ups cost nothing.
- Say it in plain language: People who do 30–60 minutes of strength training per week have 10%–20% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-exercisers; more training doesn't add extra benefit; combining with aerobic exercise gives the best results.
- Benefit: Cohort meta-analysis (observational): strength training vs none, overall mortality 10%–17% lower; dose-response shows J-shape, maximum reduction (about 10%–20%) at about 30–60 minutes/week, no further gain beyond that; both strength + aerobic vs neither, overall mortality even lower.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Momma H et al. (2022). Muscle-strengthening activities are associated with lower risk and mortality in major non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061>
- Note: Observational, self-reported exercise. Evidence for the right side of the J-curve is thin; no need to limit training on this basis. For elderly people, there are additional benefits of fall prevention and muscle preservation — that's in section 1.

### 14. Don't sit still for too long; get up and move periodically
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Zero; set a reminder.
- Say it in plain language: People who sit the longest have 2.6 times the death probability of those who sit the least; prolonged continuous sitting adds extra risk on top. But 60–75 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per day can essentially cancel out the excess risk from sitting.
- Benefit: US cohort (observational, accelerometer): sedentary total time highest quartile vs lowest, overall mortality HR 2.63; longest continuous sitting episodes highest quartile vs shortest HR 1.96 — total volume and continuous duration each independently associated. Million-person pooled analysis (observational): sitting >8 hours/day with almost no activity vs sitting <4 hours with highest activity HR 1.59; about 60–75 minutes of moderate-intensity activity daily can offset the excess sitting risk (most active group sitting >8 hours HR 1.04, not significant).
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults: A National Cohort Study. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212>; Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? A harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million men and women. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1>
- Note: Observational; the longest sitters include many sick and frail people, so HR 2.63 magnitude clearly contains reverse causation. Reliable conclusion: how much you move matters more than how long you sit; when exercise volume is sufficient, sitting risk essentially disappears. TV ≥3 hours/day risk exists at all activity levels; even the most active group only pushes the threshold to ≥5 hours (HR 1.16).

### 15. Eat less processed meat (ham, bacon, sausage, luncheon meat)
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Zero, even saves money; a small sacrifice in taste.
- Say it in plain language: People who eat the most ham, bacon, and sausage have about 20% higher death probability over the same period compared to those who eat the least; each additional daily serving of processed meat raises it by 20%.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis (observational): highest vs lowest processed meat intake, overall mortality RR 1.23, total red meat RR 1.29, unprocessed red meat RR 1.10 (not significant); another dose-response meta-analysis: each additional serving/day of processed meat RR 1.23, red meat RR 1.10.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Larsson SC, Orsini N (2014). Red meat and processed meat consumption and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. American Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt261>; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Note: Controversy. NutriRECS guidelines (Johnston BC et al. (2019). Unprocessed Red Meat and Processed Meat Consumption: Dietary Guideline Recommendations From the NutriRECS Consortium. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621>) used GRADE to rate evidence as low certainty and gave a weak recommendation to "maintain current intake." The dispute is about evidence quality, not direction — no study says processed meat is beneficial. Unprocessed red meat has small, non-significant effects; focus is on processed meat.

### 16. Drink less or no alcohol
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: A little social awkwardness; saves money.
- Say it in plain language: After exceeding 100g pure alcohol per week (about 2.5L beer), more drinking means earlier death: at age 40, drinking 100–200g per week shortens life by about six months, 200–350g by one to two years, over 350g by four to five years. For overall health, the optimal amount is 0.
- Benefit: Pooled analysis of 83 prospective studies, 600,000 current drinkers (observational): overall mortality lowest point at ≤100g pure alcohol per week (about 2.5L 5% beer or 300mL 40-proof liquor); at age 40, 100–200g/week reduces life expectancy by about 6 months, 200–350g by 1–2 years, >350g by 4–5 years (reported as life-years in original). GBD 2016: combining all health losses, the lowest-risk drinking amount is 0 drinks/week. Meta-analysis correcting for study design bias: light drinking (1.3–24g/day) vs lifetime abstainers RR 0.93 (not significant), 45–64g/day RR 1.19, ≥65g/day RR 1.35.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Wood AM et al. (2018). Risk thresholds for alcohol consumption: combined analysis of individual-participant data for 599 912 current drinkers in 83 prospective studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30134-X>; GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories, 1990–2016: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2016. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2>; Zhao J et al. (2023). Association Between Daily Alcohol Intake and Risk of All-Cause Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-analyses. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.6185>
- Note: Controversy. J-curve proponents: meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies (Di Castelnuovo A et al. (2006). Alcohol dosing and total mortality in men and women: an updated meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies. Archives of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.22.2437>) reported light drinking overall mortality up to 17%–18% lower, with men ≤4 drinks/day and women ≤2 drinks/day still showing negative correlation. Opponents argue "abstainers" include people who quit due to illness and those with generally poorer health; Zhao 2023 corrected these biases and the protective effect disappeared; GBD 2018 also rates 0 as safest. Safe reading: light drinking doesn't noticeably harm lifespan, heavy drinking definitely does; "starting to drink for health" has no basis.

### 17. Eat a small handful of nuts daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: 28 grams daily, about $30–$45 per year.
- Say it in plain language: People who regularly eat nuts have about 20% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-eaters; eating them even once a week shows a difference (about 10% lower), with the most benefit at daily consumption.
- Benefit: Two US cohorts, 119,000 people, over 3 million person-years: compared to never eating nuts, less than once per week overall mortality hazard ratio 0.93 (95% CI 0.90–0.96), once per week 0.89 (0.86–0.93), 2–4 times per week 0.87 (0.83–0.90), 5–6 times per week 0.85 (0.79–0.91), 7+ times per week 0.80 (0.73–0.86).
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine, 369(21), 2001-2011. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>
- Note: Observational cohort; nut eaters generally have better lifestyles, so numbers should be discounted. Choose unsalted, unflavored varieties; calories are not low, don't eat them as unlimited snacks.

### 18. Replace some red meat with fish and poultry
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=small dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0; this is substitution, not addition.
- Say it in plain language: Eating two additional weekly servings of processed meat or red meat raises death probability by about 3%; replacing with the same amount of fish or poultry shows no increase. This entry has one of the smaller effects in this section — worth doing if convenient, but not worth fussing over.
- Benefit: Pooled analysis of 6 US cohorts, nearly 30,000 people: each additional 2 weekly servings of processed meat, overall mortality hazard ratio 1.03 (95% CI 1.02–1.05); unprocessed red meat 1.03 (1.01–1.05); same amount of poultry 0.99 (0.97–1.02), fish 0.99 (0.97–1.01), all statistically non-significant.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Greenland P, et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine, 180(4), 503-512. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>
- Note: Two weekly servings is a very small effect; don't expect to extend life through meat substitution alone. The value of this entry is knowing which meat to choose when spending the same amount.

### 19. Replace some refined grains with whole grains
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Brown rice, oats, and whole wheat noodles are slightly more expensive; taste requires adjustment.
- Say it in plain language: People who eat an additional 90g of whole grains daily (about 3 servings) have about 17% lower death probability over the same period; benefits continue up to about 200g daily.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis (observational): each additional 90g/day whole grains (about 3 servings), overall mortality RR 0.83, benefits continuing up to 210–225g/day; another meta-analysis per additional serving/day RR 0.92.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716>; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Note: Observational; whole grain eaters are generally healthier, effect likely overestimated; high heterogeneity between studies (I² 83%). No need to switch completely — switching half already puts you on the steep part of the dose-response curve.

### 20. Drink tea three or more times per week
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $5–150 per year.
- Say it in plain language: People with a tea-drinking habit have about 15% lower death probability over the same period; starting from age 50, this averages 1.26 additional years of life and 1.41 additional years free of cardiovascular disease.
- Benefit: Chinese 100,900-person cohort, 7.3-year median follow-up (China-PAR): habitual tea drinkers overall mortality hazard ratio 0.85 (95% CI 0.79–0.90); starting from age 50, habitual tea drinkers had 1.41 more years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and 1.26 more years of life expectancy.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, 27(18), 1956-1963. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>
- Note: Controversy. Observational study; Chinese male tea drinkers have high rates of smoking and drinking; the authors corrected for this but residual confounding is inevitable. Don't drink scalding hot — see the entry on hot beverage temperature in this section.

### 21. Drink three to four cups of coffee daily, without sugar or cream
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0.15–0.30 daily if brewing yourself.
- Say it in plain language: People who drink three to four cups of coffee daily have about 17% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-drinkers.
- Benefit: Umbrella review covering meta-analyses of 201 observational studies: 3–4 cups/day vs none, overall mortality relative risk 0.83 (95% CI 0.79–0.88), i.e., 17% lower.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ, 359, j5024. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>
- Note: Controversy. The original authors note evidence is almost entirely observational, "reliable randomized controlled trials are needed to determine causality." Pregnant women and people with arrhythmia, anxiety, or insomnia issues are exceptions; adding sugar and cream negates the benefits.

### 22. Eat at least 5 servings (about 400g) of fruits and vegetables daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=medium willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $1–2 daily; time for washing and cutting.
- Say it in plain language: Each additional 200g of fruits and vegetables daily lowers death probability by about 10%; eating 5 servings daily is about 13% lower than eating only 2, with no further decline beyond that.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis (observational): each additional 200g/day, overall mortality RR 0.90, benefits continuing up to 800g/day; two large US cohorts plus 26-cohort meta-analysis: 5 servings/day vs 2, overall mortality HR 0.87; 2 servings fruit + 3 servings vegetables was optimal, no further decline beyond that.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. International Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319>; Wang DD et al. (2021). Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Mortality: Results From 2 Prospective Cohort Studies of US Men and Women and a Meta-Analysis of 26 Cohort Studies. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996>
- Note: Observational; obvious confounding (people who eat more vegetables have different income, education, and exercise levels); RR 0.90 is an upper-limit estimate. Both studies agree on "how much is enough": 5 servings, no need for 10.

### 23. Eat less ultra-processed food (chips, instant noodles, pastries, fast food)
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=yes benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Requires cooking or choosing whole foods yourself; moderate time and willpower cost.
- Say it in plain language: People who eat the most ultra-processed food have about 20% higher death probability over the same period compared to those who eat the least, and 50% higher cardiovascular death. However, the certainty of this evidence is rated low, so numbers should be discounted.
- Benefit: Umbrella review (pooling observational meta-analyses): high vs low ultra-processed food intake, overall mortality RR 1.21, cardiovascular disease death RR 1.50; evidence grades for both were "highly suggestive" and "convincing" but with low/very low GRADE certainty.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>
- Note: Controversy. The classification standard (NOVA) groups foods with very different nutritional profiles together, and overlaps heavily with sugary drinks and processed meat (items 2 and 10), making it hard to isolate independent effects; GRADE rating is low. After following items 2 and 10, the marginal benefit of this entry is uncertain; the counter-evidence is the low certainty rating in the same review itself; no original studies currently show a reverse conclusion.

### 24. Don't drink sweetened beverages, including diet versions
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0, and saves money.
- Say it in plain language: People who drink more than two cups of soft drinks daily have about 17% higher death probability over the same period compared to those drinking less than one cup monthly; diet versions are about 26% higher — even higher than sugary ones. This is mostly because people who already have health conditions switch to diet drinks, so don't conclude that artificial sweeteners are more toxic, but also don't treat them as a safe substitute.
- Benefit: European 10-country, 450,000-person cohort: more than two cups of soft drinks daily vs less than one cup monthly, overall mortality hazard ratio 1.17 (95% CI 1.11–1.22); sugary drinks 1.08 (1.01–1.16), artificially sweetened drinks 1.26 (1.16–1.35).
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Mullee A, Romaguera D, Pearson-Stuttard J, et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(11), 1479-1490. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Note: Controversy. Artificially sweetened drinks show stronger association, likely reverse causation: overweight or diabetic people switch to diet versions. But this at least shows "switching to diet makes it safe" has no evidence behind it.

### 25. Don't burn coal or wood for cooking and heating; switch to electricity or gas
<!-- cost_tag: money=high time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Rural households switching stoves and fuel cost $30–200 per year; urban residents have largely already made this switch.
- Say it in plain language: People who cook with coal or wood have about 10% higher death probability over the same period; heating with solid fuel raises it by about 14%; those who have already switched to electricity or gas have 13%–33% lower death probability than those still using solid fuel.
- Benefit: China Kadoorie prospective cohort (observational, 271,000 adults without cardiovascular disease): cooking with solid vs clean fuel, overall mortality HR 1.11; heating with solid fuel HR 1.14; those who switched from solid to clean fuel vs continued solid fuel use, cooking HR 0.87, heating HR 0.67. Outdoor PM2.5: 104-cohort meta-analysis, long-term exposure per 10 µg/m³ increase, natural-cause mortality RR 1.08.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Yu K et al. (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151>; Chen J, Hoek G (2020). Long-term exposure to PM and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.105974>
- Note: Observational; households that switch fuel tend to be wealthier, HR 0.67 contains confounding. Individuals can do little about outdoor PM2.5 (moving, masks, air purifiers); air purifiers have no mortality-endpoint studies, so no numbers are given in this section.

### 26. Let hot drinks cool before drinking; don't drink scalding tea, soup, or coffee
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0; wait two to three more minutes.
- Say it in plain language: Drinking very hot tea raises esophageal cancer probability to 8 times that of drinking warm tea; even "hot" doubles it. Drinking within two minutes of pouring raises risk to 5 times that of waiting four minutes or more. Letting it sit for two minutes cuts most of this risk.
- Benefit: Iranian esophageal cancer high-incidence area, 300 cases, 571 controls: compared to drinking warm tea, drinking "hot" tea esophageal squamous cell carcinoma OR 2.07 (95% CI 1.28–3.35), "very hot" OR 8.16 (3.93–16.9); drinking within 2 minutes of pouring vs waiting 4+ minutes OR 5.41 (2.63–11.1).
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Islami F, Pourshams A, Nasrollahzadeh D, et al. (2009). Tea drinking habits and oesophageal cancer in a high risk area in northern Iran: population based case-control study. BMJ, 338, b929. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b929>; Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, et al. (2016). Carcinogenicity of drinking coffee, mate, and very hot beverages. Lancet Oncology, 17(7), 877-878. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1470-2045(16)30239-X>
- Note: The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies beverages above 65°C as Group 2A (probably carcinogenic), while coffee itself is not carcinogenic. High esophageal cancer rates in China's Chaoshan and Taihang Mountain regions are linked to the habit of drinking while still scalding.

### 27. Go outside in sunlight during the day; don't stay indoors all the time
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0; a few minutes daily, commuting and lunch breaks are enough.
- Say it in plain language: Women who deliberately avoid sun exposure have 0.6–2.1 years shorter life expectancy compared to the most sun-exposed; the original authors' statement was that non-smokers who avoid sun have similar life expectancy to smokers in the highest sun-exposure group.
- Benefit: Swedish 29,500-woman, 20-year follow-up cohort: compared to the most sun-exposed group, sun-avoiders had 0.6 to 2.1 years shorter life expectancy; the authors wrote "non-smokers who avoid sun exposure had life expectancy similar to smokers in the highest sun exposure group."
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Lindqvist PG, Epstein E, Nielsen K, et al. (2016). Avoidance of sun exposure as a risk factor for major causes of death: a competing risk analysis of the Melanoma in Southern Sweden cohort. Journal of Internal Medicine, 280(4), 375-387. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12496>
- Note: Controversy. Sweden has high latitude and limited sunshine; conclusions can't be directly applied to China; sun-avoiders may already be frail or homebound. Sun exposure increases skin cancer risk — don't sunburn; still need shade in strong midday light.

### 28. Keep BMI between 20–25; lose weight if overweight
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=yes benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: The entry requiring the most willpower: long-term commitment to diet and exercise; maintaining weight loss is even harder.
- Say it in plain language: Death probability is lowest at BMI 20–25; at 27.5–30 it's about 20% higher, at 30–35 about 45% higher, at 35–40 nearly double. East Asians are more sensitive to overweight; each 5-unit BMI increase raises risk by about 40%.
- Benefit: Individual data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies (observational, limited to never-smokers, no baseline chronic disease, survived 5+ years): overall mortality lowest at BMI 20–25, 25–27.5 HR 1.07, 27.5–30 HR 1.20, 30–35 HR 1.45, 35–40 HR 1.94, 40–60 HR 2.76; for East Asian populations, each 5 kg/m² above 25 HR 1.39.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (2016). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1>
- Note: Controversy. "Obesity paradox" proponents (Flegal KM et al. (2013). Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.113905>) pooled to find overweight HR 0.94, class I obesity HR 0.95, i.e., mild overweight is actually slightly lower. The disagreement mainly comes from whether to exclude smokers, already-sick people, and early follow-up (seriously ill people lose weight first); Global BMI excluded them and overweight risk reappeared. Also, no randomized trial proves weight loss itself reduces overall mortality; the benefit is inferred from cross-sectional comparisons and shouldn't be treated as a "discount you can get by losing weight."

### 29. Eat chili peppers four or more times per week
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0.
- Say it in plain language: People who eat chili peppers four or more times per week have about 23% lower death probability over the same period compared to those who rarely eat them, and about one-third lower cardiovascular death.
- Benefit: Italian 22,800-person cohort, 8.2-year median follow-up, 1,236 deaths: eating chili peppers 4+ times per week vs rarely, overall mortality hazard ratio 0.77 (95% CI 0.66–0.90), cardiovascular death 0.66 (0.50–0.86).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Bonaccio M, Di Castelnuovo A, Costanzo S, et al. (2019). Chili Pepper Consumption and Mortality in Italian Adults. Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 74(25), 3139-3149. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.09.068>
- Note: Controversy. Observational study in Mediterranean diet context; chili eaters may generally eat more traditionally. People with gastroesophageal reflux, hemorrhoids, or irritable bowel syndrome will find chili aggravates symptoms — no need to force it.

### 30. Drink one to two servings of milk or yogurt daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: under a dollar daily.
- Say it in plain language: People who drink more than two servings of milk or yogurt daily have about 17% lower death probability over the same period compared to non-drinkers.
- Benefit: PURE cohort from 21 countries, 136,000 people, 9.1-year average follow-up, 6,796 deaths: more than two servings of dairy daily vs none, overall mortality hazard ratio 0.83 (95% CI 0.72–0.96, trend P=0.0052).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Dehghan M, Mente A, Rangarajan S, et al. (2018). Association of dairy intake with cardiovascular disease and mortality in 21 countries from five continents (PURE): a prospective cohort study. Lancet, 392(10161), 2288-2297. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31812-9>
- Note: Controversy. PURE included many low- and middle-income countries where being able to drink milk daily is itself an indicator of economic status; confounding is hard to fully eliminate. People who are lactose intolerant can switch to yogurt or low-lactose milk — no need to force regular milk.

### 31. Don't give up eggs, but don't eat three or four daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=small dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: $0.
- Say it in plain language: Eating half an additional whole egg daily raises death probability by about 7% over the same period; replacing that half with egg whites, fish, poultry, or nuts actually lowers all death risks. No need to give up eggs — just don't eat three or four per day.
- Benefit: US 521,000-person cohort, 129,000 deaths: each additional half whole egg daily, overall mortality hazard ratio 1.07 (95% CI 1.06–1.08); replacing half a whole egg with equivalent egg whites, poultry, fish, dairy, or nuts/legumes lowered overall mortality, cardiovascular, cancer, and respiratory mortality.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Zhuang P, Wu F, Mao L, et al. (2021). Egg and cholesterol consumption and mortality from cardiovascular and different causes in the United States: A population-based cohort study. PLoS Medicine, 18(2), e1003508. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003508>
- Note: Major controversy. Other large cohorts and meta-analyses found no association between eggs and mortality; US Dietary Guidelines have removed the daily cholesterol limit. This entry only argues against three to four per day — one a day is nothing to worry about.

### 32. Take baths when possible, don't just shower
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=medium willpower=no benefit=large dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: Water and electricity bills; about half an hour each time.
- Say it in plain language: People who take baths almost every day have about 28% lower cardiovascular event risk and about 46% lower cerebral hemorrhage risk compared to those bathing less than twice a week.
- Benefit: Japanese 30,000-person, 19-year follow-up cohort, 2,097 cardiovascular events: bathing almost daily vs less than twice weekly, cardiovascular events hazard ratio 0.72 (95% CI 0.62–0.84), coronary heart disease 0.65 (0.45–0.94), stroke 0.74 (0.62–0.87), cerebral hemorrhage 0.54 (0.40–0.73).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Ukai T, Iso H, Yamagishi K, et al. (2020). Habitual tub bathing and risks of incident coronary heart disease and stroke. Heart, 106(10), 732-737. <https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2019-315752>
- Note: Controversy. Results from Japanese population under bathtub culture; most Chinese families don't have conditions for daily baths. Water that's too hot or bathing too long is actually dangerous for elderly people and those with cardiovascular disease; Japan has many bathtub deaths among the elderly each year.
