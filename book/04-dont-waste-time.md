# 4. Don't Waste Time

This section only accounts for time: how many hours per day, week, or year you can save, or how much task completion rates improve. No conversion to lifespan or money; where money is involved, only the calculation method is given, not the conclusion. Most entries have evidence from single studies or time-use statistics, rated B or C.

### 1. Rewrite "I plan to do" as "at [time], at [location], when [situation], I will [action]"
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: 1–2 minutes per item to write an "if-then" sentence (e.g., "Tuesday at 8 AM when I sit at my desk, I'll start writing the introduction"); zero money.
- Say it in plain language: Rewriting "I want to do" into "Tuesday at 8 AM when I sit at my desk I'll start writing the introduction" significantly increases goal achievement across 94 tests, with a medium-to-large effect. You're just changing how you write one sentence — no cost, no effort.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests: forming implementation intentions improved goal achievement, effect size d = 0.65 (medium to large), covering initiating action, resisting distractions, and withdrawing from failing courses.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: Only works for things you genuinely want to do; writing more won't help if the goal itself isn't desired. Samples mostly students and health behavior studies; generalization to work settings requires caution.

### 2. Set exit conditions before starting
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: 5 minutes at project start to write "if by [date] [metric] isn't met, I'll stop"; zero money; requires some backbone to follow through.
- Say it in plain language: Write down "if by [date] [metric] isn't met, I'll stop" before you begin. The same body of research shows this format also helps people withdraw from doomed projects. What you save is all the time you would otherwise keep pouring in.
- Benefit: Implementation intention meta-analysis shows pre-set "if-then" plans also promote disengagement from failing courses of action; what's saved is all the time that would have continued to be invested.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: The disengagement effect is a sub-finding in the meta-analysis without standalone time-savings numbers. Exit conditions should be set during the calm planning stage; setting them later gets biased by sunk costs (see next item).

### 3. When deciding whether to continue, only consider future investment and future returns, not sunk costs
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: Zero money; requires accepting "the time already spent is gone."
- Say it in plain language: Money and time already invested make people want to continue — this is consistently found across 98 datasets. So when deciding whether to keep going, only calculate "how much more do I need to invest" and "how much will I get back," not what's already spent.
- Benefit: Already-invested money, effort, and time make people more likely to continue (sunk cost effect); meta-analysis of 98 effect sizes confirms the effect is stable. Avoiding it saves the subsequent time locked in by "not wanting to waste" what's already spent.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Note: Arkes & Blumer's theater experiment only verified the qualitative conclusion (people who paid more for season tickets attended more shows in the first six months); specific session numbers per group need verification. Roth et al. found the effect weakens over time and affects older people less.

### 4. Estimate project duration based on how long similar tasks actually took, not on plans
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Zero money; a few minutes beforehand to check how long the last similar task took.
- Say it in plain language: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish their thesis but actually took 55.5 days; only 29.7% finished within their estimate. Those asked to recall how long similar past tasks took before estimating raised on-time completion from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students predicted 33.9 days for thesis completion, actual was 55.5 days, with only 29.7% finishing on time per their estimate. After being prompted to link past experience to the current task, on-time completion rose from 29.3% (control) to 60.0%, and optimistic bias disappeared.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Note: Buehler's sample was Canadian university students, single study, small samples (37 and about 40 per group); reference class forecasting in engineering projects per Flyvbjerg; Halkjelsvik review notes underestimation is more common than overestimation, but effect sizes vary greatly by task and method.

### 5. Send an agenda before meetings; don't meet without one; stand up if you can
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: Organizer spends 5–10 minutes writing an agenda; stand-up meetings need participant buy-in.
- Say it in plain language: Meetings with agendas are perceived as more effective by participants. In experiments, sit-down meetings lasted 34% longer than stand-up meetings with no difference in decision quality.
- Benefit: Two surveys (958 and 292 people) found agenda use was a significant predictor of perceived meeting effectiveness; experiments showed 55 sitting meeting groups lasted 34% longer than 56 standing groups with no difference in decision quality.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Note: Stand-up experiment used 5-person lab tasks; long meetings and large conferences shouldn't be directly extrapolated. Leach's conclusion is "perceived effectiveness," not objective output.

### 6. Cut meeting count; don't meet when async text communication works
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Requires colleague and supervisor cooperation; writing a clear paragraph takes slightly more effort than speaking.
- Say it in plain language: The more meetings in a day, the more tired people feel and the higher their subjective workload. Every meeting you cut saves exactly the length of that meeting — not a minute less.
- Benefit: Among employees working 35+ hours weekly (two surveys of 676 and 304 people), meeting time demands' relationship with work attitudes and well-being was moderated by meeting quality and task interdependence. A diary study showed more meetings per day correlated with higher fatigue and subjective workload. Time saved equals the duration of the meeting cut.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Note: Controversy. Rogelberg 2006 found the meeting-frequency/well-being relationship isn't uniformly negative; in highly interdependent roles, more meetings can actually help. No direct study compares async replacement with meetings; that's the author's inference.

### 7. Break large tasks into subtasks before estimating and starting
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: Breaking down takes 5–15 minutes; zero money.
- Say it in plain language: People don't automatically break tasks into parts when estimating duration. Listing subtasks first reduces underestimation. After breaking down, each small step can start immediately, reducing procrastination.
- Benefit: People don't spontaneously decompose multi-faceted tasks into sub-parts (literature review, discussion, reference list, etc.) when estimating; listing subtasks first reduces planning fallacy. After breaking down, each small step becomes an immediately startable action, also reducing procrastination (strong predictors of procrastination include task aversiveness and delay).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Note: Kruger & Evans only verified the qualitative conclusion in the abstract, not specific percentages. Over-decomposing also wastes time; the standard is "each subtask completable in one sitting."

### 8. Set your own deadline for tasks without external deadlines
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Zero money; once set, commit to it, otherwise it's meaningless.
- Say it in plain language: Students with external deadlines finish on time 80.6% of the time, but only 38.7% finish within their own estimates. Completion time is largely determined by deadlines, not self-estimates. So for tasks without deadlines, set your own and stick to it.
- Benefit: Students with external deadlines finished 80.6% on time vs only 38.7% within their own estimates. Completion time strongly correlated with deadline (r = 0.82) but weakly with self-estimates.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Note: The study confirmed external deadlines' effectiveness; self-set deadlines (time-boxing, Pomodoro, etc.) haven't been directly tested — this is inference by analogy. Telling others about your self-set deadline makes it closer to an external one.

### 9. Use your hourly wage to decide which chores to outsource
<!-- cost_tag: money=high time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Outsourcing fees; requires one-time calculation.
- Say it in plain language: Chinese people spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes daily on housework (1 hour 59 minutes for those who actually do it). People who spend money to buy time report higher life satisfaction, and the same amount spent on time buys more immediate happiness than spending on things.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 1 hour 17 minutes daily on housework, those who do it average 1 hour 59 minutes (2024 survey, 38,500 households, 107,000 people). In a 4-country, 6,271-person sample, people who spent money to buy time had higher life satisfaction; field experiments showed buying time produced greater immediate well-being than buying material goods.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time-Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: Calculation method: hourly wage = monthly after-tax income ÷ actual monthly working hours. Outsource when the per-task cost is below your hourly wage and you'd actually use the saved time on earning or resting. Only the method is given, not the conclusion, because everyone's hourly wage and outsourcing prices differ. Whillans' outcome is well-being, not time.

### 10. Set hard limits on short videos and mindless scrolling
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: High willpower needed; requires turning off notifications, deleting apps, or setting app timers.
- Say it in plain language: Chinese people average 5 hours 37 minutes of internet use daily (6 hours 3 minutes for users), up from 2 hours 42 minutes in 2018 by the same measure. This is the largest number in this section's time account — cutting one hour gives you 365 extra hours per year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 5 hours 37 minutes of internet use daily, users average 6 hours 3 minutes, participation rate 92.9% (2024); same measure in 2018 was 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest number in this section — cutting one hour equals 365 extra hours per year.
- Evidence level: C
- Source: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time-Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). 2018 National Time-Use Survey Bulletin. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>; TODO (to verify: CNNIC 55th/56th China Internet Development Report figures on average weekly internet hours and short video user scale; official PDFs not extractable)
- Note: Internet use includes work, study, and socializing — not all waste. Rated C because no study quantifies "how much time is saved by limiting and where it goes," only aggregate statistics.

### 11. Don't watch TV and rolling news; check needed information at fixed times
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Moderate willpower; may miss some real-time updates.
- Say it in plain language: Americans over 15 watch 2.6 hours of TV daily, consuming half of all leisure time. Chinese people average 1 hour 40 minutes, with 75–84 year-olds at 3 hours 16 minutes. Check needed information at set times to reclaim this entire block.
- Benefit: Americans 15+ watch TV 2.6 hours daily, half of all leisure time (5.2 hours) (2025); Chinese residents watch TV 1 hour 40 minutes daily, 75–84 age group 3 hours 16 minutes (2018).
- Evidence level: C
- Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). 2018 National Time-Use Survey Bulletin. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Note: Only aggregate statistics; no study quantifies where the time goes after reducing TV/news. The "batch checking" approach is the author's practice.

### 12. Spend time learning keyboard shortcuts and automation for commonly used software
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=medium willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: One-time investment of a few to tens of hours; initially slower than using a mouse.
- Say it in plain language: Most long-time Word users still click the toolbar, but controlled experiments show keyboard shortcuts are the fastest of three methods. Learning once is a one-time cost; every subsequent use pays it back, and the longer you do the same type of work, the more you gain.
- Benefit: Among 251 experienced Word users, most rarely used more efficient keyboard shortcuts, relying mainly on icon toolbars; controlled experiments confirmed shortcuts were the fastest method. Time savings accumulate with daily use; people doing the same type of work long-term benefit most.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Note: Efficiency experiment had only 6 participants; abstract doesn't give seconds saved per operation. Only learn operations you use 10+ times daily — learning everything is its own waste.

### 13. Prioritize commute length when choosing where to live; shorten one-way commute
<!-- cost_tag: money=high time=medium willpower=no benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: High — may mean higher rent or smaller apartment; moving itself is time-consuming.
- Say it in plain language: The longer the commute, the lower subjective well-being, and this loss isn't compensated by higher wages or better housing. Cutting 30 minutes each way saves 5 hours per week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 50 minutes of transportation daily, participants 1 hour 2 minutes (2024). German panel data shows longer commutes systematically lower subjective well-being without compensation via income or housing. Review confirms commute satisfaction declines with duration regardless of transport mode. Cutting 30 minutes each way saves about 5 hours weekly.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>; Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time-Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: Controversy. Chatterjee et al. review notes consistent association between commuting and overall life satisfaction hasn't been established; people can generally trade better jobs or housing for longer commutes. Transportation statistics include all travel, not just commuting. Off-peak scheduling can also compress the same journey.
