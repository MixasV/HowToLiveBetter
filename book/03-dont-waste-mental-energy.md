# 3. Don't Waste Mental Energy

This section only accounts for energy and time: daily available attention, reaction speed, error rate, and recovery time after interruption. All numbers come from direct measurements in experiments or cohort studies; no conversion to lifespan or mortality. Most entries have evidence level B, which is normal for this field and doesn't mean the recommendations are invalid.

### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications and keep your phone out of sight while working
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: A few minutes to set up; almost no willpower needed.
- Say it in plain language: Even a single notification buzz — without looking or touching — significantly degrades attention task performance, by an amount comparable to actually answering a call or texting. Even just having your phone on the desk while resisting the urge to check reduces available mental capacity; the more phone-dependent you are, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Receiving notifications alone (without looking or touching) significantly degrades attention task performance, with effect size comparable to actually making calls or texting (lab study, university students); two experiments showed that even successfully resisting checking, the mere presence of your own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity, with phone-dependent people showing the largest decline.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>; Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Note: All single-session lab tasks; cumulative effects in daily work not directly measured. People who truly need to be on-call can keep notifications for only a few key contacts.

### 2. Set a fixed wake-up time, even on weekends
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; sleeping in less on weekends requires a little willpower.
- Say it in plain language: The most irregular 20% of people have circadian rhythms about 2.5 hours later than the most regular 20% — equivalent to living in permanent jet lag. Students with more regular schedules tend to have better grades.
- Benefit: 61 university students tracked for 30 days; the most irregular quintile had circadian rhythm about 2.5 hours later than the most regular quintile (melatonin onset 00:08 vs 21:32), sleep propensity peak about 1.8 hours later; sleep regularity correlated positively with academic performance (r = 0.37).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Note: Single cohort, observational, small sample. Model analysis suggests rhythm differences mainly come from different light exposure patterns, so fixing wake-up time works best combined with morning light exposure.

### 3. Sleep 7 to 8 hours per night; don't accept 6 as sufficient
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Reserving 1 to 2 extra hours daily for sleep.
- Say it in plain language: After two weeks of sleeping only 6 hours nightly, cognitive performance drops to the level of being awake for two consecutive days and nights. The problem is you don't notice: subjective sleepiness plateaus after a few days, and 6-hour sleepers rate themselves as just as sleepy as 4-hour sleepers. So "I'm fine on 6 hours" is itself a symptom of sleep deprivation.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21–38 sleeping only 6 hours nightly for 14 days saw cognitive performance decline cumulatively to levels equivalent to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation; subjective sleepiness ratings rose only in the first few days then barely increased, with no difference between 6-hour and 4-hour groups (RCT); acute sleep deprivation (up to 48 hours) effect on simple attention lapses g = -0.776, reasoning accuracy g = -0.125 (meta-analysis of 70 studies).
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>; Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Note: The most important point is that "feeling fine" is not evidence of sufficient sleep; long-term 6-hour sleepers subjectively adapt but their performance doesn't. The meta-analysis covers acute total deprivation; chronic restriction effect size comes from a single RCT.

### 4. No caffeine after 2 PM
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Zero; people who habitually drink afternoon coffee need a little willpower.
- Say it in plain language: Drinking a large coffee (400 mg) 6 hours before bedtime reduces objectively measured sleep by over 1 hour, yet sleep diaries show no detectable difference — you don't notice it yourself. A regular cup of coffee (107 mg) needs to be consumed more than 8.8 hours before bedtime to avoid affecting sleep. For an 11 PM bedtime, 2 PM is the cutoff.
- Benefit: 400 mg caffeine 6 hours before bedtime reduced objectively monitored total sleep time by 1.1 to 1.2 hours vs placebo, while subjects' sleep diaries showed no difference — undetectable by the sleeper. Meta-analysis: caffeine reduces total sleep by 45 minutes on average and sleep efficiency by 7%; a cup of coffee (107 mg) must be consumed more than 8.8 hours before bedtime to not affect sleep.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>; Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Note: Drake is a single lab study using 400 mg (about 2–3 cups of coffee). For slow caffeine metabolizers, the cutoff is earlier. The benefit dimension is sleep duration, stacking with item 3.

### 5. Check email and messages in fixed batches a few times daily
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; need to explain your response rhythm to colleagues; moderate willpower.
- Say it in plain language: Comparing the same people across two weeks: the week limited to checking email only 3 times daily showed significantly lower daily stress, with the same total emails sent and received. Without limits, people check email an average of 15.5 times daily.
- Benefit: 124 adults in a two-week within-subject comparison: the week limited to 3 email checks daily had significantly lower daily stress (Cohen's d = 0.37) than the unlimited week, with no difference in emails received or replied to; baseline email checking averaged 15.5 times daily.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Note: Single study, small-to-medium effect size, outcome is stress not productivity. Instant messaging not directly studied; only inference by analogy.

### 6. Block even brief interruptions during work requiring sustained thought
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Close the door, wear headphones, or put up a "do not disturb" sign; no money.
- Say it in plain language: An average 2.8-second interruption doubles the error rate on the next step; a 4.4-second interruption triples it. Field tracking shows interrupted tasks take an average of 25 minutes to resume, with 2.26 other tasks inserted in between. Interrupted people work faster but report higher stress, frustration, and effort.
- Benefit: Average 2.8-second interruptions doubled step error rates in sequential tasks, 4.4-second interruptions tripled them (lab); field observation of 24 knowledge workers: task topic switched on average every 11 minutes, 57% of tasks were interrupted, resuming the interrupted task took 25 minutes 26 seconds on average with 2.26 other tasks inserted; interrupted people completed faster but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort (48-person experiment).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>; Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>; Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Note: Field studies distinguish external interruptions from self-initiated internal ones; closing the door only blocks the former. Use in combination with items 1 and 5.

### 7. Do one thing at a time; don't answer messages during meetings
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: Zero; requires a little willpower.
- Say it in plain language: Every task switch slows reaction time and increases errors. People who habitually have multiple screens open haven't developed multitasking ability — they actually perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: Task switching clearly slows reactions and increases errors (review); heavy media multitaskers are more easily distracted by irrelevant stimuli and memories, and perform worse on task-switching tests (lab, university students).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>; Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Note: Specific switching cost percentages are highly task-dependent; no unified number given. Ophir is cross-sectional and can't distinguish "multitasking causes poor attention" from "people with poor attention prefer multitasking."

### 8. No glowing screens for one hour before bed; read paper books or e-ink instead
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; requires willpower.
- Say it in plain language: Reading from a glowing screen before bed delays falling asleep by about 10 minutes compared to paper, suppresses melatonin by 55%, shifts the next day's circadian rhythm by over 1.5 hours later, making mornings groggier and slower to wake.
- Benefit: Lab crossover study: reading from a glowing e-reader before bed took about 10 minutes longer to fall asleep than paper (25.7 vs 15.8 minutes), suppressed evening melatonin by 55%, delayed next-day circadian rhythm by over 1.5 hours (melatonin onset 22:31 vs 21:01), and caused groggier mornings requiring longer to fully wake.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Note: Small sample, controlled lab conditions; effects with real-world phone brightness and duration may be smaller. How much phone night mode reduces this wasn't measured in this study.

### 9. Go to bed on time; don't stay up for games, short videos, or pornography
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; requires long-term resistance to "just one more episode" inertia; keeping the phone out of the bedroom is easiest.
- Say it in plain language: In 2025, Chinese people averaged 6.97 hours of nighttime sleep with average bedtime at 0:10 AM. People using phones/tablets before bed are over twice as likely to sleep too little and 2.7 times more likely to feel sleepy during the day. Just having the device in the bedroom without using it shows similar numbers — the simplest solution is keeping the phone out of the bedroom entirely.
- Benefit: 2026 Chinese average nighttime sleep 6.97 hours, average bedtime 0:10 AM (Chinese Sleep Research Association). Pre-sleep portable device use associated with insufficient sleep (OR 2.17), poor sleep quality (OR 1.46), daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72); device merely present in bedroom without use: similar numbers (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27) (meta-analysis of 20 cross-sectional studies, 125,198 children and adolescents); among 844 adults aged 18–94, those still using phones after lights-off had slower sleep onset, lower sleep efficiency, and more daytime fatigue (cross-sectional).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>; Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>; Chinese Sleep Research Association (2026). 2026 China Sleep Health Research White Paper. <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>
- Note: Controversy. The cost of staying up late itself is in item 3; evidence for pornography's effects on ordinary people's energy and cognition lacks reliable causal data — one systematic review and meta-analysis concluded "pornography problems" stem more from personal belief-behavior inconsistency than content itself (Grubbs et al. 2019, <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>), and a 280-male study found viewing duration unrelated to partner sexual erectile function (Prause & Pfaus 2015, <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>); this entry only addresses its sleep displacement.

### 10. Don't make major decisions or send important messages late at night
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=small dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; just postpone until after a good sleep.
- Say it in plain language: People kept awake for 49.5 hours doing risk-taking decision experiments learned to avoid high-risk choices when rested but repeatedly chose high-risk options when sleep-deprived, with older people more affected. Late-night messages and decisions follow the same logic.
- Benefit: 34 healthy adults performed the Iowa Gambling Task (a risk-taking decision measure) after 49.5 hours without sleep; when rested they quickly learned to avoid high-risk options, but after sleep deprivation chose high-risk options more, with older people more affected (lab study, single study).
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Note: Studied the extreme case of staying awake for two consecutive nights; ordinary late-night stints until 2 AM haven't been directly measured. The "don't send messages" part is experiential (C); it won't hurt to wait until morning.

### 11. If sleepy in the afternoon, nap for 10 minutes, not 30
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: 10 minutes and a place to lie down or lean over.
- Say it in plain language: A 10-minute nap immediately improves alertness, with effects lasting 2.5 hours; a 5-minute nap is basically useless; a 20-minute nap takes 30 minutes to kick in; a 30-minute nap starts with a groggy period where performance is actually worse than not napping at all.
- Benefit: After restricted sleep the previous night, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, energy, and cognitive performance, with some benefits lasting 155 minutes; 5 minutes showed almost no benefit; 20-minute benefits appeared only after 35 minutes; 30-minute naps caused sleep inertia (worse performance initially) before improvement.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Note: Single lab study, healthy young adults. No need to force sleep if you can't; closing your eyes and resting for 10 minutes has no research-supported benefit on its own.

### 12. Use earplugs or find a quiet room for memory-intensive work in open-plan offices
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=small dimension=time -->
- Cost: Earplugs cost a few dozen yuan; booking a meeting room takes a little time.
- Say it in plain language: When background noise rises from 39 to 51 decibels, you remember fewer words, feel more tired, and become less motivated. Watching nature videos with sound during breaks restores energy better than continuing to listen to office noise.
- Benefit: Students working in simulated open-plan offices showed fewer words remembered, higher self-reported fatigue, and lower motivation when background noise rose from 39 to 51 dB (LAeq); watching nature videos with sound during breaks restored energy ratings better than listening to river sounds or office noise.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Note: Single lab study. The main harm comes from intelligible speech; white noise headphones help with this but weren't tested in this study.

### 13. Don't work more than 49 hours per week
<!-- cost_tag: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: Potentially less overtime pay, or the cost of negotiating with supervisors.
- Say it in plain language: WWI British munitions factory output records: up to 49 hours per week, output was proportional to hours worked; beyond 49 hours, growth rate declined; at 70 hours weekly, output was nearly identical to 56 hours — the extra 14 hours were wasted.
- Benefit: WWI British munitions factory worker output records: up to 49 hours per week, output proportional to hours; beyond 49 hours, diminishing returns, reaching maximum around 63 hours; 70 hours output nearly identical to 56 hours, meaning the extra 14 hours were pure waste.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Note: Data from manual labor, century-old factories, predominantly female workers; threshold may differ for knowledge work (the author notes other occupations may have higher or lower thresholds). The directional conclusion (beyond a point, marginal output approaches zero) is more reliable than the specific number 49.

### 14. When you notice yourself ruminating on the same bad event, switch to a hands-on task
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; requires noticing you're ruminating, which itself takes practice.
- Say it in plain language: Repeatedly replaying the same bad event won't produce answers — it worsens depression, entrenches negative thoughts, impairs problem-solving, prevents action, and exhausts people willing to listen. When you notice you're going in circles, do something that requires your hands.
- Benefit: Review: rumination worsens depression, reinforces negative thinking, impairs problem-solving ability, hinders action, depletes social support; associated with anxiety, binge eating, substance abuse, and self-harm.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Note: This is the hardest entry in this section to quantify; the review gives direction rather than numbers. "Switch tasks" is the author's experiential recommendation; the literature supports that distraction outperforms continued rumination, but no comparative studies exist on what specific task to do.

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as symptoms, not facts
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=mortality -->
- Cost: 0 yuan.
- Say it in plain language: In a 20-year twin study, each standard deviation increase in pessimism was associated with about 13% higher death probability over the same period and 20% higher cardiovascular death. Conversely, high optimism scores provided no corresponding benefit. The target is pessimism itself, not "insufficient positivity."
- Benefit: Australian 2,978 twin study, age 50+, average 20-year follow-up, 1,068 deaths: each standard deviation higher on pessimism scale, overall mortality hazard ratio 1.134 (95% CI 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵), cardiovascular death 1.196 (1.045–1.368, P=0.0093), no association with cancer death; optimism scores showed no significant association with mortality.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Note: Controversy. Observational; authors argued against reverse causation by noting cancer death wasn't associated, but residual confounding remains. Note that optimism has no protective effect, so no need to force "positive thinking" — the goal is not to treat pessimistic expectations as accurate predictions of the future.

### 16. Reduce draining relationships; learn to decline unwanted requests
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: Short-term social friction, possibly losing some relationships; requires substantial willpower.
- Say it in plain language: The damage from one draining person is larger and more consistent than the benefit from one supportive person. Subtraction first, then addition.
- Benefit: Survey of 120 widowed women aged 60–89: negative social interactions had a more consistent and stronger association with psychological well-being than positive interactions, meaning the loss from one draining person exceeds the gain from one supportive person.
- Evidence level: C
- Source: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Note: Special sample, cross-sectional data, outcome is well-being not energy; placement in this section is the author's inference. No objective standard exists for which relationships are "draining" — only personal judgment.

### 17. Fix low-value decisions like what to wear and what to eat
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=small dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; a little time upfront to set default options.
- Say it in plain language: This entry has no hard evidence. The original "making choices depletes willpower" experiment failed to replicate in two large-scale replications (2,000 and 3,000-person multi-lab studies, effect essentially zero). Fixing what you wear and eat is still worth doing — the reason is saving the minutes and second-guessing, not "saving willpower."
- Benefit: Not quantifiable. The original experiment claimed choices and self-control deplete a shared limited resource (ego depletion), but two large pre-registered multi-lab replications failed to reproduce this: 23 labs, 2,141 people, d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15]; 36 labs, 3,531 people, d = 0.06, not significant.
- Evidence level: C
- Source: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998). Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252>; Hagger et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>; Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Note: Controversy. "Decision fatigue" is treated as settled in popular writing, but lab evidence couldn't survive the replication crisis. This entry is kept only because cost is nearly zero and the time saved is real; don't expect it to improve afternoon judgment.

### 18. When angry, leave first; treat the other person like weather, not an enemy
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=medium dimension=time -->
- Cost: No money; restraining yourself from responding in the moment and walking away, then deciding later whether to address it, takes a little practice.
- Say it in plain language: Reframing what just happened from a different angle, or standing in the other person's position, both genuinely reduce emotions (the latter works better). Suppressing expression has no effect. Additionally, people who punish offenders ruminate longer, while non-retaliators move on faster — people overestimate the satisfaction of revenge beforehand.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons: cognitive reappraisal (reframing) effect on emotion d = 0.36, perspective-taking d = 0.45, distraction d = 0.27, suppression ineffective (d = -0.04); three experiments showed people who punished offenders ruminated longer, non-punishers moved on faster, and people overestimated revenge satisfaction in advance.
- Evidence level: B
- Source: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>; Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Note: Meta-analysis measures emotion intensity, not energy; effect sizes small to medium; revenge study is a single experimental series, hence B. "Empty boat" is a parable from Zhuangzi: an empty boat bumping you doesn't anger you, but a manned boat does — same collision, different reaction. This is analogy, not evidence. For actual losses, follow legal paths in sections 8 and 9 — much cheaper than reacting on the spot.

### 19. When feeling down, do the highest cost-effectiveness things first: move, get sunlight, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- cost_tag: money=0 time=medium willpower=some benefit=large dimension=time -->
- Cost: No or very little money; a few times weekly of brisk walking, jogging, or strength training, going outside in daylight, keeping regular hours, one phone call.
- Say it in plain language: For depression, brisk walking and jogging had the best effects in these 218 trials, followed by yoga and strength training; higher intensity was more effective. Light therapy works strongly for seasonal depression and also helps non-seasonal. Online self-help CBT courses work; having guidance helps when symptoms are heavier. If in crisis, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Network meta-analysis of 218 randomized trials, 14,170 people: walking or jogging effect on depression g = -0.62 (95% credible interval -0.80 to -0.45) vs active controls, yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49, with higher intensity yielding better effects. Light therapy meta-analysis: seasonal depression effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08), non-seasonal 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). Internet CBT (online self-help courses) individual data network meta-analysis: guided and unguided both superior to controls; for moderate-to-severe (PHQ-9 > 9), guided was better. 12356 national psychological assistance hotline available at least 18 hours daily.
- Evidence level: A
- Source: Noetel M et al. (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>; Golden RN et al. (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>; Karyotaki E et al. (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364>; National Health Commission (2024). Notice on using the "12356" national unified psychological assistance hotline number. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202412/content_6994470.htm>
- Note: These are adjuncts for mild-to-moderate depression; moderate-to-severe cases or those with suicidal thoughts need medical care. Call 12356 first (see section 1, item 16). Exercise trials mostly small samples without blinding; light therapy studies up to 2003, small samples; "sleep on time" and "talk to someone" are experiential with no standalone literature. Don't treat "move" as a demand on depressed people — walking for 10 minutes counts.
